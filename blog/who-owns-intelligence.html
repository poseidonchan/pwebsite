Who Owns Intelligence?

Useful AI could become part of the computer you were going to buy anyway.

Yanshuo Chen September 24, 2026 3 min read

Read the full analysis ↗ PDF · 48 pages

I want to ask AI for help before I'm ready to share my work.

Think of an unfinished research idea, a private document, or a decision you don't want to discuss with anyone yet. AI could help with all of these. But I'd rather not have to upload everything to someone else's servers first.

I think we'll reach a point where a useful AI runs on the computer you already use every day. It can help with your files, your code, and your questions while keeping that work on your machine. You can still turn to a more powerful model online when you need one. You get to choose when to do that.

It has to be useful for real work

For everyday work, I care about fairly simple things. Can the AI make a code change that passes the tests? Can it find the right answer in my documents? Can it do that quickly enough that I'd actually use it?

Making models smaller and getting more work out of the same hardware can help. A better model or software update could let your computer handle a task it used to struggle with. You might get a useful upgrade without buying a new machine.

That's why I care about efficient AI. It can put useful tools on computers more people can afford.

What would you actually pay extra?

Say you're already planning to replace your computer. You'd normally spend $1,800, or you could spend $2,000 on one that also runs the AI you need. In this example, the extra cost for AI is $200. Buying a separate $2,000 machine just for AI would add the full $2,000 to your spending.

You still have to count electricity, the time you spend setting things up, and whether running AI slows down your other work. And you only save money on online AI services if you actually spend less on them. If you keep paying the same subscription, that bill hasn't changed.

The extra $200 is a much easier purchase to consider. That's why I think ordinary laptops and desktops could bring personal AI to many more people than a separate machine bought just for AI.

NVIDIA, AMD, and Apple can all have a place

Different people will want different things from an AI computer. Here's how I see the opportunities:

  • NVIDIA: Some people will pay more to run demanding models and have the software work reliably.
  • AMD: There's room for everyday PCs that run useful AI at a price more people can afford.
  • Apple: It can make AI easy to use through the devices and apps people already know.

What would convince me is seeing people use these tools day after day without constantly fixing things. An impressive speed test only tells you so much about that. And a computer sold with AI features isn't automatically a whole new sale: the buyer may have been replacing an old laptop anyway.

I want to decide what my AI shares

I'd like my AI to start with the files on my computer. If it needs a more powerful model online, it should show me what it wants to send and ask first.

I should be able to move my saved conversations and notes to another app, switch models, remove access to a folder, or delete something I no longer want it to remember. Running on my own computer doesn't guarantee safety by itself. The software still has to protect my files and respect those choices.

If an AI is going to know my work and keep track of my life, I want the final say over what it sees, what it remembers, and what it shares.
