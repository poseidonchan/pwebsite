Who Owns Intelligence?

Personal AI may arrive with the computer you were going to buy anyway.

Yanshuo Chen September 24, 2026 3 min read

Read the full analysis ↗ PDF · 48 pages

Would you send your most private question to a cloud AI?

The more useful an assistant becomes, the more it needs to understand: unfinished research, private documents, difficult decisions. That raises a question beyond model quality. Who holds that understanding, and who decides how it can be used?

My thesis is that personal AI will become a standard capability of the computer. Better models, more efficient architectures, and improving software can bring useful private intelligence into machines people already buy for work and life. The cloud can keep extending the frontier while an increasing share of everyday work stays nearby.

The next leap could be a model update.

A personal computer never needed to outperform a mainframe to change everyday life. Local AI has a similar threshold: completing familiar work reliably, at an acceptable speed. A code change should pass its tests; a document answer should recover the evidence that matters.

Smaller active models, hybrid attention, quantization, and better runtimes can reduce the hardware needed for those results. An existing machine may become useful for a new task after a software update. That makes model design and deployment efficiency central to who can afford capable AI.

Start with the computer you would buy anyway.

Suppose you would normally buy a $1,800 work computer, but choose a $2,000 configuration to run AI locally. With otherwise comparable purchases, the additional hardware investment is $200. Buying a separate AI station creates a different financial obligation: its full purchase price is additional.

Electricity, setup, maintenance, and interference with other work still count. So does the fraction of tasks the local model can actually complete well enough to reduce a real cloud bill. An unchanged subscription is not a saving. This explains how local AI on a normal replacement PC could be worthwhile even when a dedicated station fails to pay for itself.

Several platforms can have a lasting place.

I expect different users to sustain different routes. NVIDIA can earn a premium for dependable execution of demanding models and workflows. AMD can help make sufficient intelligence ordinary across general-purpose PCs. Apple can bring it through devices, operating systems, and user relationships it already connects.

This is a thesis about differentiated coexistence. Its test is whether these platforms make useful work easier over time. Better support, reliable updates, and lower maintenance can matter as much as peak performance. Adoption also does not turn the entire existing PC market into new AI revenue.

Ownership should mean control over the next step.

The system I want can work with private material locally, then ask for outside help with my permission. I should be able to see what will leave the device, export my memory, replace a model, withdraw permissions, and recover my work. Local execution still needs security and reliable software; physical possession alone does not deliver those guarantees.

The most consequential change may be when disclosure happens. An assistant can begin helping with the material already in my hands. I decide what it sees, what it remembers, and when it asks for help elsewhere.
